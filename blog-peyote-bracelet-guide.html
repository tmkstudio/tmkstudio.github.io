Peyote Stitch Bracelet Patterns: A Beginner’s Guide to Reading Bead Charts

Peyote stitch is the classic flat bead-weaving technique behind most patterned beadwork bracelets: rows of seed beads locked together in a flexible, fabric-like band. The stitch itself is genuinely simple — pick up a bead, skip a bead, pass through the next bead. What trips up beginners is not the weaving but the chart. Peyote stitch bracelet patterns are drawn on a strange offset grid that seems to slant, the rows refuse to run in straight lines, and the first two rows are somehow also one row. This guide explains how the chart maps onto the beadwork, so you can open your first pattern and know exactly where every bead goes.

Why the chart grid looks offset

In peyote stitch, beads do not sit in neat columns like bricks in a wall; they nestle into the gaps of the row below, offset by half a bead, more like a honeycomb. Charts are drawn the way the finished beadwork actually looks, which is why the grid appears staggered. Each cell on the chart is one bead, in the colour shown. Once you accept that the slant is the stitch’s natural shape — not a printing error — the chart stops looking wrong and starts looking like a photograph of the bracelet you are about to make.

Reading the chart row by row

Here is the part nobody warns you about: the first two rows of the chart are strung onto the thread together, in one pass, before any weaving happens. You pick up all the beads for rows 1 and 2, slide them down to a stop bead, and only then begin row 3 — the first woven row — by picking up a bead, skipping one bead from the strung row, and passing through the next. The beads from your strung length settle into alternating “up” and “down” positions, and every woven row only ever goes through the up beads. From there you work back and forth across the band, turning at the end of each row, and the pattern grows in a zigzag. On the chart, that means you read rows diagonally rather than straight across. Practical tip: lay a ruler or a sticky note under the row you are working and move it down one row at a time. Because each row shares beads with its neighbours, one misread row is easy to spot early — the colours simply stop lining up.

Word charts: the beginner’s best friend

A word chart spells the pattern out in text, row by row: “Row 4: 3A, 1B, 2C, 1B, 3A”, where each letter stands for a bead colour in the key. Many beaders — including experienced ones — prefer word charts for bracelet patterns because there is no grid to misread and you can tick off each row with a pencil as you finish it. The best peyote stitch bracelet patterns include both formats for every design: the picture chart for an overview of the finished piece, and the word chart for the actual weaving. Beads and codes: why the key lists Delica numbers

Most modern bracelet patterns are designed for Miyuki Delica beads, and the colour key lists their codes (such as DB-0351) alongside a swatch and the number of beads needed in each colour. There are two reasons. First, Delicas are cylinder beads: perfectly uniform in size, unlike rounded seed beads, which vary just enough to make peyote edges wobble and patterns drift. Uniform beads are what give a finished bracelet its crisp, tiled look. Second, the codes make shopping exact. Instead of guessing which of twelve similar turquoise beads the designer used, you order the code in the key, in the quantity the key states, and your bracelet comes out looking like the photograph. If you substitute colours, swap one code at a time and keep a note — future you will be grateful.

Starting, tension and finishing

Begin by threading a stop bead about 15 cm from the end of your thread; that tail is later used to attach half the clasp. Keep your tension snug but not strained — the band should flex like fabric, and beads should click into their pockets without forcing the needle. Weave in the same direction the chart grows, and when you run low on thread, weave the tail back through the beadwork in a zigzag before adding a new length the same way; peyote has no knots to hide behind, and zigzag tails are invisible and strong. For clasps, slide clasps and magnetic clasps suit flat bands best, and most patterns state the finished length including the clasp, so measure your band before attaching it. If you are between sizes, add or remove two rows at a plain section of the pattern rather than stretching the motif.

Three tips before your first bracelet

Choose an even-count design for your first piece — the turns are simpler and the edges stay straight. Start with a pattern in two or three strongly contrasting colours, so a wrong bead announces itself immediately instead of hiding until row twenty. And stitch a short test strip of twenty rows before committing to a full bracelet: it teaches your hands the rhythm, confirms your tension, and lets you check the finished length against your wrist. With a clear chart and a word chart to follow, a first bracelet is a weekend project, not a marathon.
