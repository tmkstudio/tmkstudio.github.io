Sashiko Templates: Japanese Embroidery Patterns for Mending and Making

Sashiko — literally “little stabs” — is the Japanese tradition of decorative running stitch, worked for centuries in white thread on indigo cotton. It began as practical reinforcement: layers of cloth stitched together for warmth and strength in the cold north of Japan, and the patched, re-stitched garments known as boro. Over time the strengthening stitches settled into geometric patterns of great beauty, and those patterns are what sashiko templates capture today. A template gives you the geometry exactly — the evenly spaced waves, stars and interlocking circles that are tedious to draw by hand and unforgiving of wobbly spacing — so your stitching, not your drafting, is what shows.

The classic motifs and what they mean

Traditional sashiko motifs are drawn from nature and geometry, and most stitchers meet the same beloved set first. Seigaiha, overlapping quarter-circles, represents ocean waves and is the pattern most people picture when they hear the word sashiko. Asanoha, a six-pointed star built from triangles, is the hemp-leaf pattern, long used on children’s clothing as a wish for strong, fast growth. Shippō links overlapping circles into a lattice symbolising the seven treasures of Buddhism and endless harmony, while kikkō, a field of hexagons, echoes the tortoiseshell and stands for longevity. Ichimatsu, a simple chequerboard, and jūjihan, a cross-flower lattice, round out the classic set. Learning to recognise them is half the pleasure: once you can name the motifs, you start seeing them everywhere in Japanese textiles, packaging and architecture.

How to use a printable sashiko template

Using printable sashiko templates is a three-step job: print, transfer, stitch. Print at 100% (Actual Size) — never “fit to page” — and check the 1-inch test square printed on the sheet with a ruler; if the square measures true, the motif is at the scale the designer intended. The Tmk Studio sashiko templates are drawn at true print scale in two sizes for exactly this reason, so the same motif can cover a cushion front or sit small on a pocket without redrawing anything.

For the transfer itself, you have several good options. On light or medium fabric, tape the template to a window or light box, lay the fabric over it, and trace with a fine fabric marker or hera pen. On dark indigo, carbon transfer paper (chalk-based, in white or yellow) between template and cloth works well: trace over the lines with a stylus and the motif appears in faint chalk. The most forgiving method for beginners is printable wash-away stabiliser — print the template straight onto the stabiliser sheet, stick it to the fabric, stitch through it, then rinse it away. Whichever method you choose, test on a scrap first; five minutes of testing saves a ruined garment.

Stitching technique: the running-stitch rhythm

Sashiko uses a single stitch — the running stitch — but with a distinctive rhythm. Instead of dipping the needle in and out one stitch at a time, you load several stitches onto a long sashiko needle, using a palm thimble to push the needle through in one smooth motion. The stitches on the surface should be slightly longer than the gaps underneath — a ratio of about three to two, traditionally compared to grains of rice lying in a row. Keep your tension relaxed: pulling tight puckers the cloth and shrinks the motif. On curves, shorten the stitches a little to keep the line smooth, and at sharp points, take one tiny stitch exactly at the tip so the corner stays crisp. Aim for even, calm stitches rather than tiny ones; consistency is what makes sashiko look serene.

Sashiko templates for visible mending

The tradition’s roots in repair make sashiko a natural fit for visible mending, the modern practice of patching clothes decoratively instead of hiding the damage. Place a patch of cotton or linen behind (or over) the worn area — denim knees, shirt elbows and the inner thighs of jeans are classic candidates — pin it flat, transfer a motif across the patch and the surrounding cloth, and stitch through all layers. The running stitches both decorate and reinforce, spreading stress so the weak area stops tearing. For small holes and stains, individual motifs on stick-and-stitch sheets are even quicker: the Visible Mending Templates pair 24 stick-and-stitch motifs with boro patch sheets sized for exactly this kind of repair, so you can peel, stick, stitch and rinse without any tracing at all. A garment mended this way usually outlasts its unmended twin — and it looks better than it did when it was new.

What you need to start

The kit list is short and cheap. Fabric: plain-weave cotton or linen, indigo if you want the classic look, but any colour that contrasts with your thread works. Thread: sashiko thread is non-divisible and matte, sold in skeins; ordinary embroidery floss used whole will do for a first piece. Needles: long sashiko needles with a large eye, plus an adjustable palm thimble. Marking: a hera marker, fabric pencil or transfer paper, depending on your transfer method. And a template you trust, printed true to scale. That is all — no hoop is required, though some stitchers like one for large pieces.
